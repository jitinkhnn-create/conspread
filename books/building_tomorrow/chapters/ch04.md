The Invisible Choices

No one sees every choice we make. No one hears every thought
in your mind. No one knows all the moments when you wanted
to speak, help, forgive, include, protect, or walk away.
Yet these invisible choices quietly build our character. They
shape the kind of friend we become. The kind of citizen we
become. The kind of human being we become.
This chapter is about learning to notice those moments. The
moments when our heart whispers. The moments when
relationships test our values. The moments when the Earth
and the world ask for our care. We may not remember every
fact we learn in school.
But if we learn to choose with awareness, courage, and
kindness, we will be different people for the rest of our lives.
So take a deep breath.
Open your mind.
Listen closely.
The next lesson isn't about the world. It's about who we are
becoming.
“The Tiffin Mix-Up”
It was just after lunch when Siya opened her schoolbag and
saw it — a stainless-steel tiffin, neatly packed, but not hers.
“Oh no,” she muttered. “I took the wrong one.”

[[p.60]]
She looked inside. A single aloo paratha, some pickle, and two
Marie biscuits wrapped in tissue. Definitely not hers. Her mom
always packed colorful sabzi and a chocolate. She remembered
this morning — she had been rushing, barely looking when
she grabbed the tiffin from the counter.
She turned around, scanning the room. No one noticed.
Everyone was finishing up, chatting, and packing up their
boxes. She checked the name sticker: “Zoya – Class 6B”.
Zoya? The quiet girl. Barely talks to anyone. Always eats alone
in the corner.
Siya stood there, tiffin in hand, frozen.
Her friends were already outside near the water cooler. She
could just put it back on the bench and go. Zoya might think
she forgot it herself.
Or… she could go and give it to her.
Simple, right? But her stomach turned.
She imagined walking across the room, handing it over. What
if someone noticed? Would they tease her for talking to “that
quiet girl”? Would her friends make faces? Would Zoya think
Siya was trying to show off?
Her hand tightened around the metal box.
No one would know if I just left it there, Siya thought.
But something small inside her, like a flicker, didn’t sit right.
She turned toward the corner of the room. There was Zoya,
sitting on the last bench, water bottle in hand, quietly looking
into her empty bag.
Their eyes met, just for a second. And then Siya looked away.

[[p.61]]
What would you do?
Every day, we make so many small decisions.
What to say. What not to say.
Who to talk to. When to speak up.
What to do when no one’s watching.
Some decisions are made quickly — like choosing a pencil or
answering a question in class.
Others feel heavier — like deciding whether to help someone,
tell the truth, or stand out.
In the story above, Siya faced a small but important moment.
She could walk away and let things be. Or she could return
the tiffin to Zoya — the quiet girl no one really talked to.
Both options were possible. But only one felt… right.
Sometimes we make choices because we’re scared.
Scared of being left out.
Scared of what people will say.
That’s called deciding out of fear.
Sometimes we choose what others expect — even if it doesn’t
feel right inside.
That’s deciding to fit in or to please others.
But there’s another way to choose.
A softer, deeper way.
A way that comes from a place inside you that already knows
— even when everything outside is confusing.
That place is your heart’s voice.
It doesn’t shout.
It doesn’t always give answers in words.
But you can feel it.
It might feel like a gentle tug in your chest.

[[p.62]]
Or like a tight knot when something feels wrong.
Or a peaceful warmth when something feels right.
• Your mind is like Google Maps — it looks for the fastest,
smartest route based on other people’s roads.
• Your heart is like a compass — it quietly points you toward
what truly matters to you, even when no road is visible.
Your heart voice is not about doing what you feel in the
moment (like snapping or crying). It’s deeper than emotion —
it’s calm, kind, steady. It doesn’t need to win. It just wants to
be true.
In Siya’s story, she didn’t know what the right answer was at
first.
But when Zoya looked up, something stirred in her — a
knowing, a spark.
That was her heart speaking.
And learning to hear that… is a superpower.

Not loud like a class bell. Not sharp like a teacher’s voice.

Where Do Heart Decisions Show Up in Daily Life?
You don’t have to be in a big, dramatic moment to listen to
your heart.
Most of the time, your heart shows up in the small, everyday
choices — the ones that happen in seconds, when no one is
watching.
Here are a few real-life moments where you might hear that
quiet voice inside:
Friendships
You’re in a group chat, and someone sends a mean joke about
another kid.

[[p.64]]
Your mind says: “Just laugh. Don’t make it a big deal.”
But your heart says: “That’s not fair. I wouldn’t want this
done to me.”
•
Heart moment: Will you join in or speak up?
At Home
Your younger sibling accidentally breaks your favorite pen.
Your brain says: “Yell! That was special!”
But your heart says: “She didn’t mean to. Remember when
you broke Dad’s glasses once?”
• Heart moment: Can you stay kind, even when angry?
On Social Media or Group Apps
You want to post something that’ll get likes — but deep down,
it’s not really you.
Maybe it’s showing off or copying someone else.
• Heart moment: Is this true to who I am?
In Class
You know the answer, but you’re afraid to raise your hand.
You’re worried someone might laugh if you're wrong.
• Heart moment: Will you be brave and speak, or stay silent
and safe?
When No One’s Watching
You find money on the floor.
There’s no one around. No cameras.
Your brain says: “Keep it.”
Your heart says: “Someone probably needs this.”
• Heart moment: What will you do when no one will ever
know?

Making heart-based decisions doesn’t mean being perfect.
It means learning to pause, listen, and choose what feels most

Over time, this becomes a kind of inner strength.
The more you practice, the stronger it grows.
And that’s how we build not just a good life —

“The Birthday Seat”
It was Aarav’s birthday.
He came into class wearing a slightly-too-big denim jacket,
holding a box of éclairs to pass around. His mom had written
his name in glitter on a little badge, and he wore it proudly
on his chest.
“Happy Birthday!” shouted Anya, already dragging two desks
together near the window — the unofficial "birthday seat"
spot where the birthday kid always got to sit, surrounded by
friends.
Aarav was smiling… until he saw something strange.
Dev and Samar were already sitting in the birthday seat.

[[p.66]]
“Hey, that’s for Aarav!” someone called.
But Dev just shrugged. “We sat here first. He can sit on the
other side.”
Aarav froze.
Half the class was watching.
Dev was one of the loudest kids in class — popular, always
joking. Samar too. Aarav had always wanted to be part of their
group. This could be his chance.
He slowly walked over. “Can I sit in the middle?”
Dev looked up, smirking. “Chill, bro, it’s just a seat. Come sit
here.”
He patted the chair far away from the center.
Aarav hesitated. His best friend, Kabir, was looking at him
from behind, frowning slightly.
He could push back — tell them it was his seat today.
Or laugh it off, sit quietly, and don't cause drama.
His heart pounded.
He Had A Choice:
•
Go along with Dev to stay in the group — even if it felt
unfair.
• Or say something, even if it risks rejection.
At that moment, the “Power of We” wasn’t about everyone
being nice.
It was about what Aarav valued in relationships: respect,
space, and voice.
And whether he believed he deserved it.
What do you think Aarav should do?
We all know about being “smart” — answering questions,
solving problems, remembering facts.

[[p.67]]
But there’s another kind of smart that we don’t always learn
about in school.
It’s called relational intelligence — and it’s the superpower of
understanding people.
Relational intelligence means noticing how others feel, how
your words affect them, and how to keep relationships
healthy — even when things get tricky.
Let’s go back to Aarav’s story.
It’s his birthday.
He has the right to the birthday seat.
But when his classmates act like it doesn’t matter, he’s stuck.
Should he speak up? Stay quiet? Laugh it off?
His choice isn’t just about a seat.
It’s about something deeper:
•
How does he see himself in a group?
•
Does he believe he matters?
• How much should he adjust to keep the peace, and when
is it okay to say “no” kindly?
Relational Intelligence is about:
• Listening deeply — not just to words, but to tone, face,
and silence.
• Knowing your feelings so you don’t dump them on others.
• Understanding differences — that not everyone thinks or
feels like you.
• Balancing “Me” and “We” — respecting others and
yourself.

[[p.68]]
A Simple Way to Understand It:
•
Your brain is like a calculator — it solves numbers.
• Your relational intelligence is like a mirror and a bridge.
It reflects what’s inside you, and it connects you to others.
Relational intelligence doesn’t always mean agreeing or being
everyone's friend.
It means being aware, clear, and kind — even when things
get messy.
It helps you in groups, in friendships, in your family, and even
online.
It helps you choose your people — and not just follow the
crowd.
When you grow this smart, you don’t just win arguments—
You build trust, understanding, and a real connection.
That’s the true Power of We.




The listener? The leader? The peacemaker? The honest






How did that feel?






Relational intelligence isn’t something you “use” only when
things go wrong.
It’s part of how you walk through the world every single day
—
in texts, in classrooms, on lunch tables, in your tone of voice,
and even in your silence.
Here’s how it shows up in real life:
•
In-Class Discussions
A friend gives the wrong answer. The class giggles.

[[p.70]]
You know what the teacher is really asking.
Do you raise your hand proudly, or do you look at your
friend with kindness first?
 Relational moment: Being smart without making
others feel small.
•
On WhatsApp or Group Chats
Someone’s left unread.
A mean sticker is sent after someone shares a selfie.
The group laughs, but you feel a weird twist in your gut.
 Relational moment: Do you stay silent? Or text them
later and say, “Hey, you, okay?”
•
During Lunch Break
Your usual group is joking loudly.
A quieter classmate walks by and seems to want to join
in.
They don’t say anything.
 Relational moment: Do you notice? Do you scoot over
and make space?
•
In Family Arguments
Your sibling starts yelling.
Your parent is tired.
You feel stuck in between.
 Relational moment: Can you stay calm, speak clearly,
or even be the one who listens first?
•
When You Mess Up
You interrupt a friend. Forget their birthday. Say
something without thinking.
You feel it later — that oops feeling.
 Relational moment: Can you own it and say, “I’m
sorry— I didn’t mean to hurt you”?

[[p.71]]
•
Why This Matters
Being relationally intelligent doesn’t mean being perfect.
It means being:
Aware of how you impact others
Willing to repair when needed
Brave enough to show care and set boundaries
Relational intelligence is the kind of intelligence that makes
you someone others trust, respect, and feel safe with.
“The Broken Tap”
Every morning before school, Meher brushed her teeth while
the water ran — just like always.
But this morning, something felt different.
Outside her window, two giant water tankers rumbled past.
Her father muttered under his breath, “That’s the third time
this week. What’s going on with the supply?”
At school, Ms. Devika explained that the city was running out
of groundwater.
“Water tables are falling,” she said. “Some areas are only
getting water once a day.”
Meher blinked. Once a day?
Her own water was always there. She had a filter, a geyser,
and a running tap.
That evening, her grandmother said something Meher couldn’t
forget:
“Child, we used to walk three kilometers to fill one pot. We
never wasted a drop. This Earth doesn’t belong to us — we
belong to it.”

[[p.72]]
That night, brushing her teeth again, Meher stared at the
running tap.
Her hand reached forward.
She turned it off.
Just stood there — toothbrush in mouth, heart full of
thought.
For the first time, she wasn’t just brushing her teeth.
She was making a choice.
A quiet one.
But a powerful one.
A citizen’s choice.
What Does It Mean to Be a Conscious Citizen?
Most people think “citizenship” means having a passport or
voting when you're older.
But that’s just one part.
Conscious citizenship starts much earlier — in our daily
choices, small actions, and how we see ourselves in the bigger
world.
Let’s go back to Meher.
She didn’t give a speech. She didn’t go to a protest.
She turned off the tap — and turned on her awareness.
That’s where conscious citizenship begins:
In the moment you realize, “I’m not separate from this planet.
I’m a part of it. I belong to the Earth, and it belongs to me.”
We are all connected — to the water we drink, the trees
outside our window, the people who pick our fruits, and the
air that flows through every breath.

[[p.73]]
But many of us grow up thinking we’re just “users” —
consumers of Earth’s things.
Conscious citizenship flips that thinking:
From taking → to caring
From me only → to we all
From using the Earth → to being part of it
A Simple Way to Understand It:
•
Your brain is like a smartphone — it helps you connect.
• But your Earth-Heart is like Wi-Fi — it connects you to
everything, even the things you can’t see.
Being a conscious citizen means:

Turning off the tap not just for bills, but because
someone else might need that drop.

Picking up trash not for praise, but because this planet is
your home.

Caring about where your things come from — and where
they go.
It’s not about being perfect.
It’s about being aware, awake, and kind — to the Earth, to
people, and to life itself.
You don’t have to wait to grow up to care.
You already belong.
The Earth isn’t a thing you live on — it’s something you live
with.

Where Does Conscious Citizenship Show Up?
You don’t need to be a grown-up, an activist, or a science
genius to care for the Earth.
You’re already making choices — every single day — that
connect you to the planet.
Let’s explore where this shows up in your normal life:
1.
Brushing Teeth or Bathing
•
Tap running for 5 extra minutes = 25 litres wasted.
•
Conscious moment: Can you turn it off when not in
use? Could you challenge your family to a water-saving week?
2.
Food on the Plate
A third of all food is wasted globally.
You didn’t like the dal today, so you threw it. Someone
else didn’t eat all week.
•
Conscious moment: Could you start with less? Try a
new dish instead of rejecting it. Share extra with
someone in need?
3.
Online Shopping
Ordering a pencil box from a big website.
It comes in 3 layers of plastic + 1 cardboard box = trees
cut + fossil fuels burned.
•
Conscious moment: Could you buy local? Share with
a friend? Ask for less packaging next time?
4.
Packaged Snacks
A chip packet takes 5 minutes to finish. The wrapper will
live in a landfill for 500 years.

[[p.76]]
•
Conscious moment: Could you shift to fruits,
homemade snacks, or carry your own jar?
5.
School Projects
Making 3 chart papers for a one-day event. Glitters,
thermocol, and sketch pens used once — then trashed.
•
Conscious moment: Can you reuse, create from
waste, or go digital? Could you lead a “green
creativity” project in class?
6.
Looking the Other Way
You see someone littering, wasting water, or cutting trees.
You think, “Not my problem.”
•
Conscious moment: Could you say something? Or
choose to do the opposite — clean, save, plant,
protect?
The Real Truth?
The Earth doesn’t need more people who know stuff.
It needs more people who care enough to act.
You don’t have to be perfect.
You have to show up.
Again and again.
In small, steady ways.
That’s what citizens do.
