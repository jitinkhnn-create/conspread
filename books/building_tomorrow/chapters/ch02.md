Section-2
My Side of What Happened &
Conveying Our Perspective

•
We believe that what we see is right.
•
We believe that what we hear is correct.
• We believe that what we feel is valid.
No one is ever against us; it's just the way we see the events
happening. There is always a difference between being angry
& hurting someone. People do things in anger because we
want to empty ourselves of that uncomfortable feeling and
assume that we have a place in others' lives anyway.
Moreover, when we are upset/emotional, we do not feel what
is happening around us, and we know we are not in a position
to stop it. We do feel powerless. That is a response we feel.
We don’t have control over others' actions or ways of being.
We know our friends, relatives, and people we say are toxic,
and we can either avoid going over there or choose to learn
not to take things personally.
All that others say is not for us. Moreover, what others say or
think is not true; it is how we perceive them, and what they
think is true. They always have certain assumptions about us
based on how we have reacted in earlier conversations. They
don’t know us well.
We shall shift our efforts from analyzing others to expressing
ourselves, and shall allow others to express themselves.
Always ask, never assume, and when you follow this, you will

[[p.26]]
be at an advantage, as others will have to answer rather than
irritate you.
We easily slip into memories of what happened (past events)
and how & why it happened; we are always at the center and
see ourselves as a victim. We never see/analyze what just
happened.
We always see the event through our developed understanding
and miss the context of the people involved.
Let’s review some incidents.
We often experience our world, our emotions, and our
understanding in our own unique ways and feel their impact
on us individually, even when situations involve more than just
us. Let's see how others are seeing a particular event.
For the same, let’s use the 5 Ws: Why, What, When, Where,
Who, and How.
Let’s explore
What we see (what we interpret from the situation),
How we see (the context through which we understand what
is happening),
What really is (the actual reality of the situation), and
Most importantly, how to see in a new way.
Instance 1: (Not Knowing What to Do)
When I don’t score good marks:
My family is disappointed when I score low. They scolded me,
leaving me lost and unsure. The moment I say anything that
appears irrelevant or disrespectful. I did not score low
intentionally, yet I am told to take more tuition, study harder,
use new books, or face restrictions such as less playtime. I
know I need to improve, but I feel unable, and no one truly
listens. Over time, I start to feel like a loser and question my
worth. But struggling with marks or not doing well sometimes

[[p.27]]
does not mean I am a failure. Everyone faces challenges, and
it is okay to find some things difficult. You are not alone in
this.
It is important to remember that feeling lost or discouraged
at times like this is completely normal. Everyone faces
setbacks, but a single test or disappointment does not
determine your future. You are not alone in this, and you can
grow stronger from these experiences.
What shall I do?
When you feel lost or overwhelmed, try taking one small step.
For example, you might reach out to a teacher or a friend and
ask for help with a subject you find hard. You could also take
a short break, step outside for some fresh air, or do something
you enjoy for a few minutes. Making a simple study schedule,
setting small daily goals, or writing down your worries in a
notebook can also make things feel more manageable.
Another helpful step is to review which topics you find
hardest and start by working on just one each day. These
concrete actions can help you regain control and give you a
starting point when things feel too difficult.
First, let us understand who is involved in this situation:
Mother, Father, and Siblings, and try to see it from the
family’s perspective.
What my mother sees:
My child tries, but the results do not improve. I provide
support and comfort, but nothing works. I feel pressured, as
his father may question me. I do not know how to make him
understand. I feel helpless and sometimes put pressure on,
hoping it will help, though I know love matters more.
What my father sees:
This is not good. If my child does not study, how will he build
a future, get a job, or earn respect? I need him to understand

[[p.28]]
this is serious. I spend my hard-earned money on his
education, and I feel he does not value it. I feel helpless.
What I see (my inner voice):
What difference does it make if I scored less this time? I will
do better next time. My family does not support me. If I don’t
understand something, no one explains it to me. I feel helpless.
Everyone feels helpless, though each from a different
perspective. Approaching this feeling negatively does not help.
What is needed is a shift in thinking and response. One simple
way to shift your mindset is to practice "reframing" negative
thoughts. For example, if you find yourself thinking "I can't
do this," try changing it to "This is difficult, but I can improve
with effort and time." By noticing negative self- talk and
deliberately replacing it with a more positive or realistic
thought, you give yourself a chance to see challenges
differently and build emotional resilience.
To improve, listen to your inner voice. It is normal to feel hurt,
sad, or even frustrated when things do not go well. These
emotions can feel heavy and difficult, but you are not alone
in feeling this way.
“Mujhse nai hota”, “mai nai karpata”, “Ata hi nai hai mujhey
ye”, samajh nai ata”, “Itna dimag kahan se laun”.
Let them pass without judgment. One simple way to cope with
these thoughts is to pause, take a few deep breaths, and write
down three things you are good at or that are positive about
you. Practicing this regularly can help you remember your
strengths, calm your mind, and feel more confident, even
during difficult moments.
Constant comparison only leads to self-doubt. Growth begins
when we stop seeing ourselves as less and recognize our
strengths. Take a moment to think about one thing you did
well recently, no matter how small it may seem. You might

[[p.29]]
write it down or say it to yourself. Shifting your focus to what
you have accomplished helps build self-appreciation and
confidence.
A hero does not judge himself, but knows his strengths, works
on them, and faces challenges. Rising above difficulties and
moving forward makes someone a true hero.
For example, consider Rohan, a student who struggled with
low marks and felt discouraged. At first, he thought he was
not capable and lost his confidence. But instead of giving up,
he decided to ask a teacher for help and made a plan to study
in small steps. Over time, his marks improved, and even when
he failed again, he remembered his progress and kept going.
It is important to remember that progress can be slow and
uneven, and that is completely okay. Rohan's journey shows
that setbacks can be overcome with patience and effort.
This is the point of action.
You need to guide your inner dialogue in a constructive
direction consciously:
 I can do this if I allow myself a little more time and focus.
 I can improve. Let me read this again and try once more.
 Let me reach out to a friend or a teacher for help or
clarity.
 Let me identify exactly where I am getting stuck.
 Let me find out what I don't understand.
 Let me question what assumptions I might be making.
 Am I truly present right now, or is my mind distracted?
 How are others able to do this with ease?

What approach or method am I missing here?
The key is to shift your mindset from feeling helpless to
becoming a problem solver and to acknowledge yourself for
taking a positive, proactive approach. Seeking help is not a

[[p.30]]
weakness; it is often the first step toward reducing stress and
moving forward with clarity.
Failures are not obstacles to success; they are vital guides
that help us grow. They reveal what isn't working and
highlight what needs to change. There's often a gap between
what you're truly capable of and what you believe you can
achieve. Closing this gap starts with awareness and
confidence.
Any academic challenge is simply an outcome of a test or
situation that did not meet expectations, either yours or your
family’s. It reflects the preparation that went into it. Instead
of dwelling on the result, focus on understanding what did
not work. Every setback offers you another opportunity to
improve. You can always grow, provided you believe in it.
You are given multiple chances to move forward. What
matters is an honest assessment of where you need to
improve. Identify the areas that require support, and actively
seek the right guidance and resources to strengthen them.
Ultimately, your attitude toward failure defines your progress.
Your willingness to learn from it and turn it to your advantage
matters far more than the number of attempts you make.
Setbacks may occur along the way, but they are part of the
learning process and help you prepare better for success.
Avoid comparing yourself with others, as it often leads to
unnecessary disappointment. Instead, focus on your own
journey, what you have, what you can improve, and how you
can move forward with greater clarity and confidence.
Activity: “5W Mind Map & Role- Play”
Step 1: Draw a central bubble with the problem (for example,
“Low Marks”) and create branches for Why, What, When,
Where, Who, and How.

[[p.31]]
Step 2: Write what each family member (mother, father, self)
thinks or feels about the situation.
Step 3: Work in pairs to role- play the scenario. One person
plays a family member, and the other responds with a calm,
solution-focused approach.
Step 4: Reflect on what new responses or actions could
improve the situation.
Step 5: Write one positive change you will apply the next time
you face a similar challenge.

I often feel this way, but I adjust because these people are

Instance 3: When People Make Fun of Me
People sometimes make fun of my way of being and, at times,
even of my body image. It feels irritating and hurtful, as it
seems they do not consider what I might be going through.
In such moments, I feel embarrassed, humiliated, negative,
and emotionally distressed.
In today’s environment, there is often an unspoken
expectation of perfection, whether in appearance, behavior, or
lifestyle. We are constantly exposed to comparisons, and over
time, we begin to compare ourselves to others, often unfairly.
First, let us understand who is involved in this situation,
friends and siblings, and examine it from their perspectives.
What my friends/siblings may see:
They may consider it harmless fun, without realizing its
impact. They may believe that pointing out differences is
normal or acceptable, and if someone feels hurt, they may
assume it is an overreaction. Their comments are often based

[[p.33]]
on surface-level observations rather than a deeper
understanding.
What I see (my inner voice):
“I am being made fun of.”
“I am like this, and that is why I am being targeted.”
“This is disrespectful and insulting.”
“Everyone is laughing at me.”
“I need to prove myself or respond strongly, but I don’t know
how.”
“Maybe I should ignore it, but I still don’t feel good.”
“I am not less than anyone… or maybe I am.”
What actually happens:
Others make a comment, but I assign meaning to it and begin
to believe that what they say defines me. I start focusing on
their actions and expect them to change, rather than
strengthening my own response. This shifts my energy away
from self- growth and toward self-doubt.
Who am I at that moment?
I feel weak and defensive, trying to protect myself rather than
understand the situation and learn from it. However, this
moment can also become an opportunity to accept reality,
build inner strength, and grow into a more confident version
of myself.

Instance 4: When Facing Rejections in Life
I am facing repeated rejections, and it is becoming
overwhelming. I am not selected for school games, I struggle
to make friends, and I often feel left out during group
activities or project planning.
My teachers seem disappointed with my academic
performance, and I find it difficult to participate effectively
or achieve the expected results. My struggle with
mathematics continues, and with important career decisions
approaching, I feel uncertain about which path to choose.
Constant comparison with others makes me feel left behind.
Rejection seems present in many areas of my life, sometimes
even unnoticed.
How do I overcome this? I want to succeed, to stand out, and
to create my own place.
To address these challenges, let us first understand who is
involved in this situation, which includes almost every area
and person in my life, and then try to examine it from their
perspective.
What others may see:
“You are not being included because you may not yet fit into
the current requirements or expectations. It is not about who

[[p.35]]
you are as a person, but about who best fits the role or task
at that moment. We may not yet know your strengths well
enough, or we may be focused on achieving certain results.
You may get another opportunity next time.”
What I see (inner critic): “Why me? Why am I not included?
Am I not smart enough? I must be a failure. Why is it easy
for others? I'm tired of trying. I want to give up.”
What is the reality:
Rejection is not a judgment on my worth; it is feedback about
my current readiness. It relates to preparation, skill, timing,
or fit.
Each rejection highlights areas for growth and improvement,
guiding my future preparation.
Instead of failure, see rejection as redirection, a chance to
know myself, build skills, and move forward.
Growth comes not from being accepted, but from learning,
adapting, and persevering after setbacks.
Now, I choose to feel defeated or to use these experiences as
steps toward growing stronger, more capable, and more
confident.
There is no one way to live life. Life is to live, experience,
understand for oneself, and develop oneself to create something
for oneself and the community we live in.

The Real Fight: Within the Mind
We all love to win. We celebrate victories in sports, challenges,
or situations where we can clearly see the opponent before
us. These wins make us feel strong and capable. However,
what we often overlook is that such victories are secondary.
There is another, more important fight, one that happens
within us. This inner battle shapes who we truly become.
While we may win external challenges, we often lose this
internal fight without even realizing it. Only a few develop the
awareness to observe it and consciously choose to win it. This
is the mental fight.
There have been many times when I lost this inner battle,
again and again, without even recognizing it. It did not feel
significant at the time because I was unaware of it. But once

[[p.40]]
I became aware, I realized how deeply it was affecting my
confidence, my decisions, and my growth.
This is the fight with our own mind. At times, the mind limits
our potential by justifying failures, offering reasons for
inaction, or creating a sense of comfort in avoiding challenges.
It holds us back from becoming stronger versions of ourselves.
The mind’s first response is often to:

Seek immediate and easy solutions.

Accept what others say without reflection.

Form opinions about ourselves based on others’ views.

Judge others quickly.

Avoid asking for support.

Interpret situations in ways that feel comfortable rather
than truthful.

See everything in extremes, right or wrong, mine or
others.

Expect happiness from outside, instead of building it
within.
Winning this mental fight begins with awareness. It requires
accepting situations as they are, without unnecessary
expectations, and focusing on our own growth.
True strength lies not in defeating others, but in
understanding and mastering our own minds.

Step 1: Create two columns on a page titled “What I Feel” and
“What Others Might Really Mean.”
Step 2: List your initial reactions (for example, “I feel ignored”
or “I feel taken advantage of”), and then use the 5W’s (Why,

[[p.41]]
What, When, Where, Who, and How) to explore alternative
interpretations.
Step 3: In small groups, practice a dialogue in which you
express your feelings calmly and ask clarifying questions such
as “Why did you say that?” or “What did you mean?”
Step 4: Role-play different scenarios to practice staying calm
and not taking things personally.
Step 5: Write a personal affirmation that you can use
whenever you feel overwhelmed by others’ actions.
Our internal dialogue constantly reflects on everything

and consider how others might view the same situations
