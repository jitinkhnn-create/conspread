Section-6
Tiny Steps Create Big Changes!!!

By the time you finish reading this page, your mind will have
already processed hundreds of thoughts. Some thoughts may
be about school, friends, family, social media, games, exams,
your future, or something that happened earlier today. Most
of these thoughts arrive automatically. We rarely stop to ask
where they came from or whether they are helping us become
the person we want to be.
Think about a normal day in your life. You wake up, check your
phone, talk to people, attend classes, watch videos, play
games, listen to music, scroll through social media, and have
conversations with friends and family. It may seem like
nothing extraordinary is happening. Yet, something very
important is taking place. Every experience is quietly shaping
the way you think, what you believe, the choices you make,
and the person you are becoming.
Many people believe that their future will be decided by one
big event. They think success will come from one great
opportunity, one important examination, one special talent, or
one lucky moment. However, life rarely works that way. The
truth is that our future is often built by the small things we
do repeatedly. A single workout does not make someone
healthy. A single page does not complete a book. A single
practice session does not make someone a great musician.
Growth happens through small actions repeated consistently
over time.
Imagine two students who start the year together. Both have
similar abilities. One student decides to spend just fifteen

[[p.82]]
minutes every day learning something new, asking questions,
reading, observing, and improving. The other student keeps
waiting for the perfect time to start. At the end of the year,
the difference between them may seem surprising. The
difference was not created in one day. It was created through
hundreds of tiny decisions that nobody noticed at the time.
Life is very similar. The thoughts we repeat become beliefs.
The beliefs we hold influence our actions. Our actions become
habits, and our habits slowly shape our character. This process
happens so gradually that we often do not notice it. Yet,
whether we realize it or not, we are becoming someone every
single day.
Today's world makes this even more important. Never before
in human history have young people had access to so much
information. Within minutes, you can watch videos from
different countries, learn from experts, listen to opinions,
receive advice from artificial intelligence, and communicate
with people across the world. This is an incredible opportunity,
but it also comes with a challenge. Not everything we hear is
true. Not everything we see is helpful. Not everything we
believe helps us grow.
That is why learning to think clearly, understand your values,
manage your emotions, choose your influences wisely, and
make thoughtful decisions has become one of the most
important life skills of our time. The future will not only
belong to people who know information. It will belong to
people who know how to think about information.
This chapter is not about changing your entire life overnight.
It is about understanding the small things that quietly shape
your future. It is about becoming aware of your thoughts,
habits, values, emotions, friendships, and the information you
allow into your mind. Most importantly, it is about discovering

[[p.83]]
that meaningful change does not begin with giant leaps. It
begins with tiny steps.
As you read this chapter, remember one simple idea: the
person you become tomorrow is being shaped by the choices
you make today. Every thought, every action, every question,
and every decision is like a small vote for the person you are
becoming. The good news is that no matter where you are
today, you can begin taking better steps.
Tiny steps may seem small, but over time they create big
changes.

Once we begin noticing ourselves, another question appears:
How do we know whether what we think and believe is
actually true?
Becoming A Truth Detective
Let's dive deep into various aspects that require attention and
what can serve as tools for a better tomorrow.
1.
Critical Thinking Is:
Critical thinking means carefully evaluating information
before accepting it as true. Critical thinking is like being

[[p.85]]
a detective. You don't just believe everything you hear;
you look for clues and evidence to find out what's true.
Example: Just like you read food labels to know what's
healthy, you should also check information before
believing it.
It starts with asking the 5 aspects: When, why, where,
how, and what. It would open new avenues and new ways
of looking at the same situation.
In the time of AI and Technological devices, we have to
be ready to counter the effects of AI
2.
Teach Fact-Checking Techniques
Identify Reliable Sources
Explain What Reliable Sources Are: Look for information
from credible sources such as educational institutions,
reputable news organizations, and official websites.
Example: A website ending in .edu, .gov, or .org is usually
more reliable than a random blog or social media post.
Cross-Check Information
•
Compare Multiple Sources: Compare information
from different sources to see if it matches. Example:
If you read something interesting online, check a few
other websites to see if they say the same thing.
Verify with Experts
•
Seek Expert Opinions: Consult experts or trusted
adults when unsure about the accuracy of
information. Example: If you read something about
science, ask your science teacher if it's true.

[[p.86]]
3.
Encourage Skepticism and Questioning
•
Ask Questions: Ask who, what, where, when, why, and
how about the information they come across.
Example: When you see a news story, ask yourself:
Who wrote this? What is their source? Why are they
saying this?
Healthy Skepticism
•
Promote Healthy Skepticism: Be cautious and do not
accept everything at face value, especially if it seems
too good to be true. Example: If something sounds
too amazing or scary, it’s a good idea to double-check
it before you believe it.
4.
Discuss Media Literacy
Recognize Bias and Persuasion
•
Explain Bias: Learn to recognize bias and understand
that
people
or
organizations
might
present
information in a way that supports their own views
or interests. Example: If a company tells you their
product is the best, remember they are trying to sell
something, so they might not tell the whole story.
Identify Persuasive Techniques
•
Spot Persuasive Language: Remember, advertisements,
social media posts, and articles might use persuasive
language to influence their opinions. Example: Ads
often use words like 'amazing' or 'best' to make you
feel excited about their product. Look for facts rather
than just emotions.
5.
Practical Exercises and Activities
Fact-Checking Activities
•
Interactive Fact-Checking: Engage in activities where
they practice fact-checking, like verifying a news

[[p.87]]
story together. Example Activity: Find a news story
online, then work together to identify three
additional sources that confirm or contradict it.
Debate and Discussion
•
Host Debates: Do debates on various topics where
they have to present evidence to support their points.
Example Activity: Choose a topic such as 'Should kids
have homework?' and have them find facts to support
their arguments.
6.
Model Critical Thinking and Fact-Checking
•
Demonstrate Fact-Checking: Regularly demonstrate
how you verify information in your daily life. Example:
When you see a headline online, say, 'Let’s check if
this is true by looking it up on a few reliable news
sites.'
Discuss News and Information Together
•
Shared Media Consumption: Watch the news or read
articles together and discuss the content critically.
Example: After reading a news article, ask, 'What do
you think about this? How can we find out if it’s
true?'
7.
Teach the Risks of Misinformation
•
Discuss
Potential
Harms:
Explain
how
misinformation can lead to wrong decisions, fear, or
the spreading of false news. Example: Believing and
sharing false health information can lead people to
do things that harm them rather than help them.
•
Case Studies: Talk about a viral hoax that caused
panic or a false health claim that led to people
getting sick.

Values: Your Internal Compass
Values also influence how we see the world. Values play a
crucial role in shaping our lives, guiding our behavior, and
influencing our decision-making processes. They serve as a
moral compass, helping us determine what is important, right,
and meaningful. Here’s a detailed look at the role of values in
life, especially in the context of accepting and believing
information.
The Role of Values in Life
1.
Guiding Principles
•
Moral Framework: Values provide a framework for
making ethical decisions. They help us discern right
from wrong and guide our actions in various
situations.
Example: If honesty is a core value, a person will
strive to be truthful in their interactions, even when
it’s challenging.
2.
Influencing Behavior
•
Consistent Actions: Values influence how we behave
consistently across different aspects of life, from
personal relationships to professional conduct.

[[p.89]]
Example: A person who values kindness will likely
volunteer to help others and show empathy in their
daily interactions.
3.
Shaping Identity
•
Sense of Self: Values contribute to our sense of
identity and self-worth. They reflect what we stand
for and who we are as individuals.
Example: Someone who values education may
prioritize learning and self- improvement, seeing
themselves as a lifelong learner.
4.
Determining Priorities
•
Decision-Making: Values help us prioritize our time,
energy, and resources. They influence what we
consider important and worth pursuing.
Example: If family is a core value, a person might
prioritize spending time with loved ones over career
advancement.
The Role of Values in Accepting and Believing Information
1.
Filtering Information
Bias and Preferences: Our values act as filters through
which we evaluate and interpret information. They can
create biases that influence what information we accept
or reject.
Example: If environmental conservation is a value, a
person may be more likely to accept information from
sources that support green initiatives.
2.
Critical Evaluation
Questioning Relevance: Values help us assess the
relevance and importance of information. We are more
likely to scrutinize information that conflicts with our
values.

[[p.90]]
Example: A person who values justice may critically
evaluate news stories about social issues to ensure they
align with their understanding of fairness.
3.
Influencing Beliefs
Aligning with Values: Information that aligns with our
values is more likely to be believed and integrated into
our worldview. Conversely, information that contradicts
our values may be rejected or questioned.
Example: Someone who values health and wellness may
readily accept scientific information about the benefits
of exercise and healthy eating.
4.
Promoting Integrity
Consistency with Values: Accepting and acting on
information that aligns with our values promotes
integrity and authenticity. It ensures that our beliefs and
actions are consistent with who we are.
Example: If transparency is a value, a person may favor
open and honest communication and be wary of
information from sources that seem secretive or
misleading.
Practical Applications and Examples
Educational Setting
• Teaching Critical Thinking: Educators can teach students
to evaluate information based on their values. For
instance, in a classroom discussion about ethics, students
can be encouraged to consider how different values
influence their acceptance of various viewpoints.
Personal Relationships
• Building Trust: In relationships, shared values can
strengthen trust and understanding. When partners or

[[p.91]]
friends share core values, they are more likely to believe
in and support each other’s perspectives.
Conclusion
Values play a fundamental role in shaping our lives by guiding
our decisions, influencing our behavior, and forming our
identity. When it comes to accepting and believing
information, values act as filters that help us evaluate its
relevance, credibility, and alignment with our core beliefs. By
understanding and reflecting on our values, we can make more
informed, consistent, and ethical choices in both our personal
and public lives. Our values influence our decisions. And our
decisions influence how we treat people.
Understanding ourselves, questioning information, living by
our values, and building healthy relationships are not things
we do once.
They are tiny steps we practice every day.
But how shall I deal with unfamiliar people in life?
Here’s an approach with practical steps and conversations to
help you navigate these feelings and improve friendship:
Step-by-Step Guide
1.
Self-Reflection
Identify the Problem: Think about specific behaviors or
situations that make you feel that the people around you
are toxic or not understanding.
Example:
Problem: They always make fun of me and never listen
when I try to tell them something important. It makes
me feel sad and unimportant. Communicate your feelings
to your friends assertively and calmly.
When you talk to your friends, try saying, 'I feel hurt
when you make fun of me because it makes me feel
unimportant.'

[[p.92]]
2.
Use “I” Statements:
Encourage the use of I statements to express feelings
without sounding accusatory.
Example: I feel sad when you don't listen to me because
it makes me feel like my opinions don't matter.
3.
Set Boundaries
Define Acceptable Behavior: Set clear boundaries with
their friends. It's important to let your friends know what
kind of behavior is not okay with you. You can say, 'I don’t
like it when you make fun of me. Please stop.
4.
Choose Friends Wisely
Evaluate Friendships: Think about which friendships are
positive and which might be causing more harm than
good. Think about the friends who make you feel happy
and those who often make you feel sad. Who do you think
you should spend more time with? Spend more time with
friends who are supportive and understanding. Try
spending more time with friends who listen to you and
make you feel good about yourself.
5.
Develop Social Skills
Practice Empathy: Understand others' perspectives to
improve mutual understanding. Sometimes, it helps to
think about why your friends might be acting the way
they are. Maybe they don't realize they're hurting you.
Improve
Communication
Skills:
Role-play
various
scenarios to practice handling conflicts and expressing
feelings effectively. Let's pretend I'm one of your friends.
Practice telling me how you feel when I don’t listen to
you.

[[p.93]]
6.
Seek Support
Talk to a Trusted Adult: Talk to a teacher, parent, or
another trusted adult if you need more help. If you feel
like you need more help with your friends, it's okay to talk
to your teacher or parent.
Sample Conversations
Conversation 1: Child with Parent
Child: My friends are so mean sometimes. They never listen
to me.
Parent: Can you tell me more about what actually happened?
Child: They always make fun of me and ignore what I say.
Parent: That is hurtful. I understand. How do you feel when
they do that?
Child: I don’t know, but I feel really sad and like they don't
care about me.
Parent: I understand. It's important to let them know how you
feel. You can say, 'I feel sad when you make fun of me because
it makes me feel like my opinions don't matter.'
Child: But what if they don’t listen? I know they will not listen
to me on this, either.
Parent: If they don’t listen, it might be helpful to spend more
time with friends who do listen and make you feel good about
yourself. You deserve friends who respect and understand you.
Conversation 2: Child with Teacher
Child: Mam, I feel like my friends are always ignoring me and
making fun of me.
Teacher: Ok. It’s important to feel respected and valued by
your friends. Have you tried talking to them about how you
feel?
Child: No, I'm afraid they’ll laugh at me.

[[p.94]]
Teacher: It can be hard to speak up, but sometimes friends
don’t realize they’re hurting you. You can try saying
something like, 'I feel upset when you ignore me because it
makes me feel left out.' Would you like me to help you with
this?
Child: Yes, please. I need some help. Will you talk to them for
me?
Teacher: No, Let’s practice together on how to put our point
differently, and I’ll be here to support you.
Conversation 3: Child with Friend
Child: Hey, can we talk for a minute?
Friend: Sure, please tell.
Child: I feel really hurt when you make fun of me. It makes
me feel like you don’t care about my feelings.
Friend: I didn’t realize you felt that way. I’m sorry. I was joking
around.
Child: I know you didn’t mean to hurt me, but it still makes
me feel bad. Can we try to be more respectful to each other?
Friend: Yeah, I can do that. Thanks for telling me.
Conclusion
Improving a situation involves self-reflection and effective
communication. This would help you navigate your social
relationships more confidently and in a healthier way.
